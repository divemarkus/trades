Ingenuity used a specialized **lithium-ion battery system** designed for:

* extreme cold
* ultra-low atmospheric pressure
* minimal weight
* autonomous operation on Mars

The battery pack was:

| Specification     | Details                       |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Battery chemistry | Lithium-ion                   |
| Cell count        | 6 Sony commercial-style cells |
| Configuration     | 3s2p (3 series, 2 parallel)   |
| Nominal voltage   | ~11–15 volts system range     |
| Battery capacity  | ~35–40 Wh                     |
| Weight            | Extremely lightweight         |
| Charging source   | Solar panel above rotors      |

# The actual cells

NASA/JPL engineers reportedly used:

* High energy-density **Sony lithium-ion cells**
* Similar to advanced commercial cylindrical Li-ion technology

Public reporting strongly suggests cells related to the:

* Sony/Murata VTC family
  or comparable aerospace-qualified Li-ion cells.

NASA often adapts:

* commercial lithium-ion chemistry
* then heavily validates and modifies it for space use.

---

# Why lithium-ion?

Because Ingenuity had brutal constraints:

* Entire aircraft weighed only ~1.8 kg (4 lbs)
* Mars atmosphere is only ~1% of Earth’s density
* Every gram mattered

Lithium-ion offered:

* highest practical energy density
* rechargeable cycling
* good power delivery

Without Li-ion, powered flight on Mars likely would not have been feasible at that weight.

---

# The REAL challenge was not flying…

It was surviving the Martian night.

Mars nighttime temperatures can reach:

*−80∘C to −100∘C*

That is devastating for batteries.

So most of Ingenuity’s stored energy was actually used for:

* heaters
* electronics survival
* thermal management

—not flying.

# Flight performance

Despite its tiny battery:

* Ingenuity greatly exceeded expectations
* Originally planned for only ~5 flights
* Ultimately flew 72 times

This became one of NASA’s most successful tech demonstrations.

---

# Why the battery system was impressive

The battery had to survive:

* repeated deep cold cycles
* radiation exposure
* dust
* charging variability
* vibration during launch

while remaining:

* lightweight
* high power
* reliable

That’s extraordinarily difficult in aerospace engineering.

---

# Interesting engineering detail

Ingenuity’s rotor system spun extremely fast because Mars air is so thin:

*Rotor speed≈2400–2900 RPM*

That’s much faster than most helicopters on Earth.

The battery had to deliver very high burst power during takeoff and maneuvering.

# Silicon-anode batteries have tradeoffs

The reason silicon batteries are difficult is:

* silicon expands dramatically during charging cycles

Potential swelling can reach:

*≈300% expansion potential in silicon structures*

Amprius uses:

* silicon nanowires
* engineered structures

to reduce cracking and degradation.

This is one reason the company is considered technologically interesting.

All of that means:

> bigger and/or better batteries.

# Why Amprius-like batteries are relevant

## The single biggest constraint is mass

On Mars:

* air density is only ~1% of Earth’s
* lift is extremely difficult

That means battery mass is brutally important.

Higher energy density batteries directly enable:

*Higher energy density⇒longer range + larger payloads*

This is why silicon-anode batteries are interesting.

# But Mars is harder than drones on Earth

The challenge is not just flight endurance.

The battery must survive:

| Requirement                | Difficulty |
| -------------------------- | ---------- |
| Deep cold                  | Extreme    |
| Radiation                  | Extreme    |
| Vacuum cruise to Mars      | Severe     |
| Multi-year degradation     | Severe     |
| Launch vibration           | Severe     |
| Autonomous fault tolerance | Severe     |

This is where space qualification becomes enormously expensive and slow.

A battery that works great in:

* military drones
* HALE UAVs
* aviation

may still fail space qualification.
