# Shaun Walker’s **“A War Foretold”** — Complete Summary and Analysis

This is an exceptionally important piece of investigative journalism. [The Guardian's full investigation](https://www.theguardian.com/world/ng-interactive/2026/feb/20/a-war-foretold-cia-mi6-putin-ukraine-plans-russia?utm_source=chatgpt.com) is based on **more than 100 interviews with senior intelligence officials, military figures, diplomats, Ukrainian officials, and insiders across multiple countries**.

Its central argument is striking:

> **The United States and Britain correctly understood that Vladimir Putin was preparing for a full-scale invasion of Ukraine—while much of Europe, and even Ukraine's own leadership, did not believe it. But Western intelligence simultaneously made a major mistake of its own: it expected Russia to win quickly.**

So this was both **one of the great intelligence successes of modern history** and **a collection of intelligence, political, diplomatic, and psychological failures**. ([The Guardian][1])

# 2. The beginning: when did Putin decide to invade?

One of the most interesting conclusions is that the CIA **does not know with certainty exactly when Putin made the final decision**.

However, analysts looking backward believed the likely turning point was in the **first half of 2020**.

Several events later appeared to form part of the same larger picture:

* Putin changed Russia's constitution, allowing him to remain in power beyond 2024.
* COVID isolation increasingly separated him from normal political interaction.
* He became increasingly immersed in Russian history and his own historical legacy.
* The Belarusian protests weakened Alexander Lukashenko and made Belarus increasingly dependent on Moscow.
* This created the possibility of using Belarusian territory as an invasion route.
* The Kremlin poisoned opposition leader Alexei Navalny.
* Putin increasingly embraced the ideological argument that Ukraine was not a genuinely separate historical nation.

At the time, these looked like separate events.

**In hindsight, intelligence analysts began seeing them as pieces of a larger transformation in Putin's worldview and strategic planning.** ([The Guardian][2])

# 3. The first warning signs: spring 2021

In spring 2021, Russian forces began massing near Ukraine and in occupied Crimea.

Initially, the possibilities considered were more limited:

* another Donbas escalation;
* formal annexation of additional territory;
* a southern offensive;
* perhaps a land corridor linking Donbas to Crimea.

The truly extraordinary scenario—a full invasion aimed at **Kyiv and regime change**—was not yet universally accepted.

The US received intelligence suggesting Putin might use a major speech in April 2021 to justify military action. President Joe Biden became sufficiently concerned that he personally contacted Putin and proposed a summit.

Putin's subsequent speech was less aggressive than expected, and Russia announced the end of some exercises. The June 2021 Biden-Putin summit in Geneva also appeared relatively calm.

For a moment, it looked as if diplomacy had worked.

But Walker's investigation suggests that this was misleading.

**Putin may already have decided that diplomacy was not going to resolve the Ukraine question on his terms.**

---

# 4. The turning point: autumn 2021

By September and October 2021, the intelligence became much more alarming.

Russia began another military buildup, but this time the scale and pattern were different.

US analysts began seeing indications that went far beyond a limited Donbas operation.

The key question became:

## Was Putin actually planning to take Kyiv?

That sounded almost absurd to many policymakers.

A full-scale invasion of Europe's second-largest country seemed irrational, extraordinarily risky, and economically catastrophic for Russia.

That became one of the biggest analytical problems.

### Many people effectively reasoned:

> "Putin wouldn't do something that irrational."

But the intelligence increasingly suggested:

> **He was going to do exactly that.**

# 5. William Burns goes to Moscow

Biden dispatched CIA Director William Burns to Moscow in November 2021.

Burns had previously been US ambassador to Russia and knew Putin's system well.

The purpose was essentially to deliver a warning:

* The US knew what Russia was preparing.
* A major invasion would bring severe consequences.
* Putin should reconsider.

But Putin was increasingly isolated and inaccessible.

Burns ultimately communicated with Putin remotely rather than having the kind of personal interaction that would once have been possible.

The warning did not change Putin's course.

And when Burns returned, US intelligence concern became even greater.

The internal alarm bells were now ringing loudly.

([The Guardian][1])

# 6. CIA + MI6: why were they so confident?

This is perhaps the most technically interesting part of the investigation.

Walker specifically challenges the simplistic idea that:

> "The CIA found Putin's invasion plans."

According to the account, it was **not one magical source or a single document**.

Instead, intelligence agencies assembled a mosaic.

### The evidence included:

| Intelligence source                 | What it revealed                                                                    |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 🛰️ **Satellite imagery**           | Massive Russian troop movements and equipment concentrations                        |
| 📡 **Signals intelligence**         | Military communications and operational activity inconsistent with normal exercises |
| 🪖 **Military preparations**        | Troop deployments that made little sense unless military action was planned         |
| 🕵️ **Intelligence reporting**      | Activity by Russian and pro-Russian networks inside Ukraine                         |
| 👥 **Reservist preparations**       | Expansion and preparation of forces inside Russia                                   |
| 🌍 **Multiple independent sources** | Separate streams of information pointing toward the same conclusion                 |

The US and UK also apparently had access to extremely valuable signals intelligence through:

* the **NSA** in the United States;
* **GCHQ** in Britain.

Walker reports that some evidence may have come from intercepts involving the Russian military's Main Operations Directorate—the part of the General Staff involved in strategic planning.

This is particularly important.

## The intelligence did not necessarily need a "super-spy" sitting beside Putin.

Putin could compartmentalize his intentions, but a large invasion requires thousands of people and organizations to perform preparatory tasks.

Someone has to:

* move fuel;
* move ammunition;
* deploy troops;
* activate logistics;
* prepare hospitals;
* coordinate transport;
* issue operational orders;
* prepare intelligence networks;
* mobilize reserves.

**You can hide the strategic decision, but preparing a 150,000-plus-man invasion creates enormous physical and electronic signatures.**

That is the deeper intelligence lesson.

([The Guardian][2])

# 7. Was there a CIA mole inside the Kremlin?

Possibly.

Bob Woodward had previously referred to a human source in the Kremlin, and the CIA historically had important Russian sources.

Walker notes that the CIA had previously exfiltrated a valuable source connected to Putin's foreign-policy environment.

However, the investigation suggests that **a single mole is unlikely to have been the sole explanation**.

In fact, one source summarized the core intelligence picture bluntly:

> **“No human source detected.”**

That doesn't mean there were no human sources.

Rather, it suggests that:

* HUMINT may have provided corroboration;
* technical intelligence may have supplied the strongest evidence;
* satellite imagery and intercepted communications provided independently verifiable indicators.

This is a fascinating example of modern intelligence fusion.

# 8. The Americans and British try to convince Europe

In November 2021, US Director of National Intelligence Avril Haines briefed NATO intelligence chiefs.

MI6 chief Richard Moore supported the assessment.

The response?

### Widespread skepticism.

Some European intelligence services and governments believed:

* the Americans might be exaggerating;
* Russia was engaged in coercive diplomacy;
* a strong NATO reaction could actually provoke Putin;
* Washington could be interpreting ambiguous intelligence too aggressively.

There was another huge factor:

# Iraq.

European governments remembered the 2003 invasion of Iraq and the catastrophic failure involving claims about weapons of mass destruction.

That experience had badly damaged confidence in American intelligence claims.

So when Washington said:

> "We have intelligence that Russia is preparing a major invasion."

Some people essentially thought:

> **"We've heard this before."**

The Iraq War created a kind of institutional skepticism that, ironically, made it harder for the US to persuade allies when the intelligence was actually correct.

---

# 9. The extraordinary decision: declassify intelligence

Biden's administration made a highly unusual decision.

Instead of simply keeping intelligence classified, it began selectively **declassifying information and sharing it with allies and the public**.

This was unprecedented in scale.

The goal was strategic.

## The US wanted to deny Putin several advantages:

### 1. Strategic surprise

If everyone already knew Russia's plans, Moscow could not suddenly manufacture a justification and surprise the world.

### 2. A false-flag narrative

The US publicly exposed possible Russian attempts to create pretexts for invasion.

### 3. Alliance fragmentation

Washington wanted European governments to understand why the US believed the invasion threat was real.

But there was a major risk:

**Every intelligence release risked exposing sources and methods.**

So the US established processes allowing intelligence agencies to review what could safely be released.

According to Walker's reporting, officials would sometimes receive classified intelligence briefings and then see substantially similar information appear publicly hours later.

This was a major evolution in intelligence warfare.

---

# 10. The British role was critical

Britain was not simply following Washington.

MI6 and British military intelligence independently saw evidence consistent with a coming invasion.

As part of the Five Eyes intelligence relationship, the UK also had extensive access to US intelligence.

Britain became one of the strongest voices warning:

> **This is not just posturing. Russia is preparing for war.**

British Defence Secretary Ben Wallace traveled to Kyiv and warned Zelenskyy.

His message was essentially:

> Start preparing now.

But Kyiv remained unconvinced.

([The Guardian][3])

# 11. Why Zelenskyy did not fully believe it

This is probably the most politically sensitive section of the investigation.

The article does **not** simply portray Zelenskyy as foolish.

His reasoning had a strategic logic.

Ukraine had already been at war with Russia since 2014.

Every year involved:

* Russian threats;
* military activity;
* intelligence warnings;
* political pressure;
* fears of escalation.

So another Russian buildup was not automatically seen as proof of a nationwide invasion.

Furthermore, Zelenskyy feared that publicly declaring:

> "Russia is about to invade."

could itself cause:

* capital flight;
* panic;
* bank runs;
* economic collapse;
* mass migration;
* political instability.

Ukraine was already under enormous pressure.

From Kyiv's perspective, the warnings could actually become self-fulfilling.

The Ukrainian leadership apparently considered the maximum likely scenario to be something like:

> A major escalation in Donbas.

Not:

> Russian missiles hitting cities across the country, airborne troops, armored columns, attacks from Belarus, and a drive toward Kyiv.

([The Guardian][3])

# 12. But Ukrainian intelligence agencies were seeing disturbing signs

Ukraine's intelligence services were not blind.

The SBU observed increased Russian intelligence activity.

Russia appeared to be:

* recruiting Ukrainians;
* approaching lower-level officials;
* attempting to build networks;
* cultivating people who could potentially support an invasion;
* developing what could effectively become a **fifth column**.

Some of these contacts reportedly occurred abroad, including in locations such as Turkey and Egypt.

The concern was that Russia was preparing not just a military operation but a **political takeover infrastructure**.

That is critical.

The invasion plan apparently depended on the assumption that Russia could quickly:

1. defeat Ukrainian military resistance;
2. seize Kyiv;
3. remove or neutralize the Ukrainian leadership;
4. install a new government;
5. activate pro-Russian political and intelligence networks.

In other words:

## Putin wasn't planning a long war.

He was planning a rapid decapitation operation.

# 13. The fundamental Western intelligence failure

Here is where Walker's investigation becomes particularly interesting.

The CIA and MI6 were correct about:

* the invasion;
* the scale;
* the danger;
* the likelihood that Kyiv would be targeted.

But they were wrong about something equally important:

# They expected Russia to win.

The prevailing assumption was that the Russian military would overwhelm Ukraine.

This assumption was not completely irrational.

Russia had:

* a much larger military;
* overwhelming missile capabilities;
* massive artillery;
* air power;
* armored formations;
* decades of Soviet military infrastructure;
* numerical superiority.

Ukraine's military had improved dramatically since 2014 but was still significantly smaller.

So the prediction was:

> Russia invades → Kyiv falls relatively quickly → Zelenskyy's government collapses or relocates → Russia installs a compliant regime.

That did not happen.

This is a reminder that:

## Intelligence agencies are better at identifying capabilities and intentions than predicting human behavior under extreme stress.

# 14. Putin's own intelligence failure

Perhaps the largest failure of all occurred inside Russia.

Putin appears to have constructed an information system in which **people were afraid to tell him the truth**.

His small inner circle reinforced assumptions that:

* Ukraine was weak;
* Ukrainian identity was artificial;
* Zelenskyy would flee;
* Ukrainian political elites could be bought or intimidated;
* Russian forces would be welcomed or at least tolerated;
* Western governments would eventually accept the new reality.

This was catastrophically wrong.

The Russian system had apparently become vulnerable to the classic authoritarian problem:

> **The leader receives information that confirms what the leader wants to believe.**

---

# 15. Even Russian officials were surprised

One of the most remarkable aspects of the story is how few people apparently knew the full plan.

Putin kept the operation extremely compartmentalized.

Even senior figures were uncertain about:

* whether there would be a limited operation;
* whether Donbas would be formally annexed;
* whether Russia would launch a larger offensive;
* whether Kyiv was actually the objective.

The Kremlin Security Council meeting on 21 February 2022 is particularly revealing.

On television, senior officials appeared to support Putin.

But behind the scenes, Walker reports:

* confusion;
* fear;
* uncertainty;
* opposition from some individuals.

Dmitry Kozak, Putin's Ukraine point man, reportedly argued against the invasion, warning that it would be disastrous.

Yet the public performance projected unanimity.

This is classic authoritarian decision-making.

### Privately:

> uncertainty and disagreement.

### Publicly:

> unanimous support for the leader.

# 16. The Stalin 1941 parallel

One Russian insider reportedly compared the atmosphere to the period before Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941.

Stalin had received warnings that Hitler was preparing an invasion.

But Stalin did not want to believe them.

The similarity is fascinating.

In 1941:

> Stalin ignored intelligence that contradicted his worldview.

In 2022:

> Putin apparently ignored intelligence and warnings that contradicted his worldview.

The irony is profound.

A historical pattern repeated itself inside the Kremlin.

# 17. Ukraine finally begins quiet preparations

Some Ukrainian military intelligence officers and military leaders began preparing quietly.

Under the cover of training exercises, Ukraine:

* rented safe houses;
* prepared contingency arrangements;
* secured cash;
* developed emergency plans.

But the preparations were limited because the political leadership still feared:

* panic;
* economic damage;
* political destabilization.

The result was a dangerous gap.

The intelligence services and military were increasingly worried, while the political leadership remained reluctant to behave as though war was certain.

---

# 18. The final days: Europe still didn't fully believe it

Even in the final hours before the invasion, skepticism remained.

France and Germany apparently accepted that some military action might occur.

But they still did not necessarily believe:

> Russia would launch a full-scale invasion aimed at Kyiv.

This is remarkable because by this point:

* US intelligence was convinced;
* British intelligence was convinced;
* Polish intelligence was convinced;
* Russian troops were in position;
* attack preparations were visible;
* intelligence agencies were warning that orders had been given.

Yet the full scenario still seemed too irrational to believe.

This was perhaps the article's deepest theme:

# Sometimes the failure is not lack of information.

Sometimes people have the information and reject the conclusion because the conclusion seems impossible.

---

# 19. Eight hours before the invasion

The intelligence picture had become extremely clear.

Attack orders had reportedly been detected.

Foreign journalists were already receiving warnings from intelligence contacts.

Yet there were still people physically present in Kyiv who apparently did not fully appreciate that the invasion was imminent.

Germany's BND intelligence chief Bruno Kahl, for example, was in Kyiv shortly before the invasion.

The situation changed so rapidly that he reportedly became trapped by events and required evacuation.

This symbolized the larger European failure:

> **The war was happening, but psychologically many people were still in the pre-war world.**

# 20. The invasion begins—and the predictions start collapsing

On 24 February 2022, Russia launched the full-scale invasion.

The CIA and MI6 had been right.

But almost immediately, another intelligence assumption began failing.

Russia did **not** achieve its expected rapid victory.

The reasons were numerous.

### Russian failures

* poor logistics;
* inadequate planning;
* corruption;
* unrealistic timelines;
* poor coordination;
* weak communications;
* overconfidence;
* insufficient preparation for sustained resistance.

### Ukrainian successes

* decentralized resistance;
* experienced military units;
* rapid adaptation;
* effective use of Western weapons;
* strong national identity;
* local knowledge;
* effective intelligence.

And then there was one critical decision:

# Zelenskyy stayed in Kyiv.

That decision fundamentally changed the political psychology of the war.

Russia's assumptions apparently included the possibility that:

* Zelenskyy would flee;
* the Ukrainian government would collapse;
* leadership could be rapidly replaced.

Instead, Zelenskyy remained.

Ukraine did not experience a political decapitation.

The state continued functioning.

The military continued fighting.

And Russia's "three-day war" became a grinding multi-year conflict.

# The biggest intelligence lesson

I would summarize the entire investigation this way:

## **The CIA and MI6 correctly answered the question: "What is Putin going to do?"**

But multiple governments failed to accept the answer.

And Western intelligence was much less accurate when answering:

## **"What will happen after Putin does it?"**

That distinction is enormously important.

| Question                                       | Result                       |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Will Russia invade?                            | **CIA/MI6: largely correct** |
| Will Kyiv be a major objective?                | **Correct**                  |
| Will the invasion be large-scale?              | **Correct**                  |
| Will European governments believe the warning? | **Often no**                 |
| Will Zelenskyy fully accept the warnings?      | **No**                       |
| Will Russia win quickly?                       | **Wrong**                    |
| Will the Ukrainian state collapse?             | **Wrong**                    |

# My interpretation: why this story is historically important

This investigation is not simply about Ukraine.

It is about the limits of intelligence in the 21st century.

The intelligence agencies actually performed extraordinarily well in one respect.

They saw something coming that:

* seemed irrational;
* contradicted conventional geopolitical assumptions;
* would have enormous consequences;
* was actively being denied by the target;
* was dismissed by allies.

And they were right.

But intelligence cannot force political leaders to believe it.

Nor can intelligence perfectly predict what happens after the first shot is fired.

The article demonstrates at least **five major lessons**.

### 1. Intelligence failure is sometimes a failure of imagination

The evidence was there.

The problem was:

> "Putin wouldn't do that."

He did.

### 2. Authoritarian systems can produce catastrophic self-deception

Putin's system apparently filtered out inconvenient information and amplified assumptions consistent with his worldview.

### 3. Past intelligence failures can damage future intelligence

The Iraq WMD disaster created skepticism that made it harder for Western governments to accept accurate intelligence about Ukraine.

### 4. Modern warfare is difficult to hide

A large invasion generates:

* satellite signatures;
* electronic communications;
* logistics movements;
* troop concentrations;
* reserve activation;
* political activity.

Modern intelligence can assemble these signals into a highly accurate picture.

### 5. Prediction is harder than detection

It was possible to see the invasion coming.

It was much harder to predict:

* Ukrainian morale;
* Zelenskyy's behavior;
* Russian incompetence;
* the resilience of the Ukrainian state;
* how badly Putin had misjudged his own military.

# My bottom line

Shaun Walker's investigation describes a remarkable chain of failures and successes:

> **Putin correctly hid the invasion from much of his own government, but could not hide the enormous physical and electronic preparations from Western intelligence.**

> **The CIA and MI6 correctly identified the coming war, but struggled to convince allies and Ukraine's political leadership that the intelligence was real.**

> **Putin correctly understood that he could surprise much of Europe politically, but catastrophically misunderstood Ukraine militarily and psychologically.**

> **And the CIA and MI6, despite correctly predicting the invasion, made their own major analytical mistake by assuming Russia's military superiority would produce a quick victory.**

That is why the article is more than an account of **"how the CIA knew."**

It is really an examination of the entire intelligence cycle:

**Collection → Analysis → Warning → Political Acceptance → Preparation → Prediction of Outcomes.**

The remarkable thing is that the system succeeded brilliantly at the first three stages—and then encountered major failures in the last three.
